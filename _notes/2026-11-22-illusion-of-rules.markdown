**Illusion of rules**


There's a certain pervasive sense of rules when practicing any mindful endeavor. Maybe to any endeavor at all, I'm kind of realizing now. But it certainly extends to the mindful ones.

It's not any big conspiracy. The rules are just there. If you're out grocery shopping people expect you to not lunge at them or make any 'surprising moves', and you know that, and you respect it. Whatever you do, it always feels like there's a set of rules. Even when I'm home alone, I can be pretty damn weird, but I know that shouting or stomping are probably things best not included in this game.

Similarly, when you meditate, there's a way to do it. Sure, you might call it technique – I call it rules. Because if you weren't following the description of the technique, it would be hard to say that you're doing said technique, right? Then they are rules, aren't they?

If you analyze the subject matter too hard, it becomes quite slippery. You hit nuances like 'principles' – these act like rules, yet are in fact not rules. You also realize that rules do not actually have to be followed (this explains crime!). If there are implicit rules and you don't have to follow them, are they really rules, or are they maybe just principles?

Semantics aside, what is the essence of what I'm saying here? We tend to find rules even in the absence of them, and mindfulness is one big trap for this. While acting like there are no rules, we (okay, at least *I*) hold a whole lot of rules for ourselves. We are supposed to be here, now, and accepting. This is easily expanded into subcategories, like a nauseating fever dream. When not accepting, we must accept the non-acceptance, because otherwise we are just creating more denial of the present moment. But we must be careful so that we're not trying to accept the non-acceptance out of yet another level of resistance. I could go on. Similarly, we should be present. When you are deep in thought you are usually not particularly present (come on, if I said 'boo!' while you were deep in thought you would probably recoil from surprise). It's very easy to start judging yourself when your mind starts drifting, telling yourself "I shouldn't do that." Why not? Well, you are obviously not present, which you want to be. So if you want to be present, you should obviously not be engaging in those reflexive unconscious behaviors.

Then you have that one lovely, most desired experience, and you see that what you've been doing has nothing to do with the real thing. It is instead cheap mimicry of it, even when you do your best not to do the very tempting action of splitting the Isness. The problem is that you're doing your best, or rather, doing at all. The difference between "the real thing" and whatever the hell we're doing is like the difference between dancing and performing a dance routine.

Still though, would you believe me if I walked back my claim that doing is the problem, because in the real state, doing is not forbidden at all? You can do as much as you please. I promise.

This is because the rules we commonly ascribe to mindfulness are not rules. Nor are they even principles, really. They are just a characterization of the state; they don't get you there. Yet I doubt that you would get there if you didn't know them. Either way, after such a wonderful experience, you will go straight back to your favorite rules.

Now I'm not saying you should abolish the rules. I love and am reminded of the saying "Learn the rules like a pro, so you can break them like an artist." In this case, though, I will argue that there's not much to learn. Nor technically rules to follow. But it is imperative to your success that you learn them.
