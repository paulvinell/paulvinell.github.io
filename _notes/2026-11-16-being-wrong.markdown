**Avoiding the pain of being wrong**


I think people create future pain for themselves when they untruthfully conceptualize painful situations. For instance, it may be easier to get over a breakup by convincing yourself that your ex is evil. But what happens when you encounter evidence that runs contrary to your conceptualizations?

|| High attachment | Low attachment |
|-|-|-|
| **Falsey** |Painful change|Painless change|
| **Truthy** |No need for change|No need for change|
{: .row-labels}

If one wants to minimize suffering, low attachment to your conceptualizations is ideal, but in its absence it helps to strive for truthfulness.
{: .table-caption}

When held lightly, being wrong is about as dramatic as making a correction to your grocery list. A nice place to be, if your goal is to minimize pain.

However, when held strongly, you are suddenly faced with a more pressing dilemma. Were you to accept that you are wrong, you would quickly face other facts that you managed to avoid with your untruthful conceptualization. E.g. if "my ex was evil" and I encounter evidence that calls that into question, then I must face uncomfortable dilemmas and thoughts that I could avoid by holding said belief – such as, "was it really the right move to break up?" or "maybe there's something wrong with me that causes all my relationships to go up in flames."

The dilemma is then to accept the evidence (or at least examine your beliefs accordingly) or create more delusional conceptualizations. The ideal choice, methinks, is to face the evidence honestly. But, you can, of course, choose not to. You are then officially building a house of cards. But the thing about houses of cards is that they don't have to collapse, you just have to be really careful. However, just because it doesn't collapse doesn't mean that it won't be a lot of effort keeping it standing.

When you choose the route of denial, for every piece of evidence that counters your belief, you create some kind of rationalization or slight non-painful (mostly synonymous here with falsey) modification to your world view to accomodate. It's a real commitment. The inability to face smaller pains does not negate them, but pushes their realization forwards in time. A misstep could be costly, and could send the whole house of cards into collapse. However, it would be a lie to say that it's inevitable. The main argument against this behavior is according to me that we often actually know that we're engaging in self-deception. This means that it's a sort of conspiracy by you against yourself, and the problem with conspiracies is that they take work. You will unconciously devote mental energy to maintain this illusion. In this sense, it's about as genius as taking a huge mortgage against your house, living like a king, and hoping you die before they come to collect. Feasibly – it might work! The problem, I think, is that much like a loan there is an interest to pay, and this is unfortunately automatically drawn from your bank account. Moreover, this energy deficit is part of what feeds depression and anxiety, in my view. Much like ordering with Klarna then, the economical choice in the long run is most often to pay upfront.

Notwithstanding the fairly unlikely scenario that you manage to keep your house of cards standing, there will likely be an eventual collapse. After all, if your conceptualizations aren't factual, you risk sooner or later running into evidence proving the contrary. And when the facts become inescapable, the sum total suffering and pain is swiftly unleashed, like a cascade. Honestly, that might be the best and/or worst thing that can happen to someone in this situation.

I think the tendency to choose rationalization after rationalization is part of the reason why some people become delusional to the point where others take notice. In other words, denial of reality can only go so far before others feel like you're living somewhere else.
